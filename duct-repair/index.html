Duct Repair in Austin, TX

Duct repairs for loose, leaking, crushed, or disconnected runs in Austin.

Call (512) 635-7760 Ask a question

We hear from homeowners who say one bedroom is always hot, the air barely comes out of a ceiling vent, or the house feels dusty whenever the blower starts. Those are everyday descriptions of ductwork trouble: a loose connection, a split flex duct, a crushed run, an open return, or insulation that has pulled away from the duct.

We also get calls about a rattling sound above a hallway ceiling, a vent that suddenly stopped blowing, or cool air pouring into an attic instead of a living room. A duct run carries conditioned air between the air handler and each register, so a gap at a collar or a damaged section can affect one room while the rest of the system appears normal.

A duct repair should not wait when a room has little airflow, a return is pulling attic dust, or a disconnected run is dumping cooled air outside the living space. The longer a damaged duct stays open, the more conditioned air, insulation performance, and blower capacity are wasted.

Hot room and rattling vents

We often hear, "My bedroom is hot but the rest of the house is cold," especially when a branch duct has come loose from its ceiling boot or a flex duct has been pinched in the attic. Air may still reach the register, but the reduced opening leaves the room with weak airflow.

Another common call is, "The vent is making a flapping or rattling noise." A loose metal damper, detached insulation sleeve, or unsupported flex duct can move when the blower comes on, creating a sound that seems to come from the grille or the ceiling cavity.

Some callers report dust around a supply vent, an attic smell when the AC runs, or a return grille that seems louder than before. An open return seam can draw dusty attic air into the ductwork, while a split supply duct can push air and insulation fibers into spaces around the run.

We also check vents that have stopped blowing altogether, because a collapsed flex section, a disconnected collar, and a closed or blocked damper can feel identical from inside the room. These faults require different materials and different amounts of access work, so we inspect the duct before tying the repair to a particular scope.

Ductwork inspection

We begin with a ductwork inspection that commonly takes about thirty to sixty minutes for an accessible problem. We identify the affected register and return grille, inspect the air handler connections, and follow the branch run through the attic, crawlspace, garage, or ceiling cavity where access allows.

We check flex duct for tears, compression, kinks, missing insulation, and sagging sections. At collars, boots, plenums, and takeoffs, we look for separated joints, failed mastic, loose straps, and foil tape that is no longer holding the seam. We can also measure airflow and static pressure in inches of water column when the symptom points to a restriction or a larger duct issue.

On an accessible visit, we can usually reconnect a loose run, secure a collar, seal a reachable seam with mastic, replace damaged straps, or cut out and replace a short split section of flex duct. We verify airflow at the register after the repair and check that insulation covers the repaired section.

If the damaged run is buried beneath decking, passes through a tight ceiling cavity, has several failed branches, or needs a larger section rebuilt, we explain the route and schedule the additional ductwork work separately. A replacement section may also need to be ordered when the existing duct diameter, insulation rating, or metal fitting is not available for the visit.

Attic access, flex duct, and collars

We look first at access because an exposed duct in a walkable attic is a different repair from a run above a finished ceiling or behind stored items. A narrow attic hatch, low roof framing, wet insulation, and a long route to the damaged branch all add labor to reach the collar, boot, or split section safely.

The duct material and extent of the damage also affect the work. A loose collar may need sealing and a new mechanical connection, while crushed flex duct may need a new section, support straps, insulation, and a properly sized connector. Sheet-metal trunks, return plenums, and custom transitions can require different fittings than a standard flex run.

Older ductwork can reveal more than one weak point once the insulation sleeve is opened. If several seams have failed, a return box has deteriorated, or a branch diameter is incorrect, we explain which repair addresses the immediate airflow problem and which additional work would prevent the same loss from returning.

If a fitting or specialty duct material has to be ordered, the scope changes from a straightforward accessible repair to a return visit with the correct part. When an alteration requires a permit or inspection, we also account for the access, material, and sign-off steps involved.

Air handler label and attic hatch

Before calling, we recommend noting which room has the weak, hot, noisy, or dusty vent and whether it is a supply register or return grille. If the system is running, tell us whether air is coming from the register, whether the blower sound changed, and whether the issue affects one room or several rooms.

The model number on the air handler data plate, usually found on the blower cabinet or indoor unit, helps us understand the equipment serving the ducts. A photo of the ceiling vent, attic hatch, visible flex duct, or disconnected collar can also help us bring suitable tape, mastic, straps, and fittings.

Let us know where the attic hatch, crawlspace door, garage access, or mechanical closet is located and whether storage blocks the route to the duct run.
