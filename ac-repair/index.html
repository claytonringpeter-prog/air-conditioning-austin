AC Repair in Austin, TX

Warm air, water leaks, noises, and other AC faults checked in Austin.

Call (512) 635-7760 Ask a question

Warm air from the supply vents, a rattling sound at the outdoor unit, a damp patch below the air handler, or an electric bill that climbs while the thermostat setting stays the same are all signs that an AC component needs attention. We trace the fault through the thermostat, indoor equipment, condenser, wiring, drain line, and refrigerant circuit rather than treating every no-cooling call as the same problem.

A system may run constantly without lowering the room temperature when a capacitor is weak, a contactor is worn, a blower motor is struggling, or the refrigerant charge is outside the equipment specifications. Water around the indoor cabinet can come from a blocked condensate drain, a frozen evaporator coil, or a cracked drain pan. A clicking breaker, burnt odor, or buzzing electrical compartment calls for a different check than a unit that simply has weak airflow at one register.

AC repair should not wait when there is a burning smell, repeated breaker trip, water escaping from the drain pan, ice on the refrigerant line, or no cooling during hot weather. Those conditions can damage the compressor, ceiling material, wiring, or coil if the unit continues to run.

Warm air and water leaks

Customers often call us because the AC is blowing warm air, the outside unit is humming but the fan is not turning, the thermostat screen is blank, or water is dripping through a ceiling near the indoor unit. Other common descriptions include a loud bang when the condenser starts, a grinding blower sound, short cycling every few minutes, and one bedroom that never reaches the thermostat setting. Each symptom points us toward different components, from a capacitor or fan motor to a control board, float switch, drain trap, or refrigerant leak.

A warm vent does not identify one failed part, and a puddle beneath the air handler does not always mean the drain line alone is blocked. We inspect the equipment and take readings before recommending a repair, because faults that look alike from the room can require very different parts and labor.

Condenser and air-handler inspection

At a repair call, we start with the thermostat setting, return-air temperature, supply-air temperature, filter condition, breaker, disconnect, and visible wiring. We inspect the condenser fan, contactor, capacitor, compressor terminals, indoor blower, evaporator cabinet, condensate pan, safety switches, and drain route. We use a meter to check the 24-volt control circuit and component readings, and we take refrigerant pressure and temperature measurements when the sealed system needs evaluation.

Initial electrical and airflow checks commonly take 20 to 30 minutes, while a refrigerant diagnosis or an intermittent control fault can take longer because the system must run through a cooling cycle. Items such as a capacitor, contactor, float switch, clogged drain line, or accessible wiring connection can often be handled during the same visit when the correct part is available. A failed compressor, leaking coil, damaged circuit board, or part that must be ordered may require a return appointment after the equipment and replacement part are confirmed.

Capacitor access and circuit boards

The part that has failed is a major factor in the repair scope. An accessible capacitor or contactor in the condenser compartment is a different job from a compressor electrical failure, an evaporator coil leak, or a circuit board with heat damage. Older equipment can also have discontinued controls or matching parts that need to be located before work is scheduled.

Access changes the work as well. A condenser behind a locked gate, an air handler in a tight attic, a drain line routed through a wall, or a control board behind a sealed panel adds time for safe entry and testing. If a specific motor, board, coil, or factory control has to be ordered, we confirm the model and part match before the repair is completed.

Model plate and thermostat

Before calling, check the thermostat display and note whether the indoor blower, outdoor condenser fan, or neither unit is running. The model and serial plate is usually on the side of the outdoor condenser or inside the air-handler cabinet, and a clear photo helps us identify the correct capacitor, motor, board, or refrigerant information.

Tell us which room has warm vents, where water is appearing, and whether a breaker has tripped or the equipment is making a new sound. If the air handler is in an attic, garage closet, or ceiling space, let us know about the access opening and any stored items around the panel. Have the filter size available if you know it, since a restricted filter can affect airflow and coil temperature.
