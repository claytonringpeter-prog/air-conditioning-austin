Air Conditioner Installation in Austin, TX

We install properly sized AC systems for homes across Austin and nearby areas.

Call (512) 635-7760 Ask a question

Turning the thermostat lower, replacing a clogged filter, and checking the breaker are sensible first steps when a house feels warm. A blocked filter can reduce airflow at the supply registers, and a tripped breaker can stop the outdoor condenser without warning. Those checks have limits, though: they cannot correct an undersized system, a failed compressor, a leaking evaporator coil, or equipment that has reached the end of its workable life.

We look at the whole cooling setup rather than treating the outdoor unit as the only part that matters. The condenser, indoor coil, blower, return duct, condensate drain, line set, disconnect box, and thermostat all need to suit the home and work together. Installing a larger unit without checking the return air path or duct capacity can leave rooms unevenly cooled and can make the system cycle too quickly.

A planned air conditioner installation can wait long enough for a proper survey when the existing system still cools, because equipment size, electrical connections, and drain routing need to be matched before replacement. A system that will not run should not be left through rising outdoor temperatures, especially when a stalled condenser or overflowing drain is already affecting the home.

AC won't cool or keeps running

Customers often call because the AC will not cool, warm air is coming from the vents, or the outdoor unit will not start while the indoor blower still runs. Other common descriptions include a system that keeps running without lowering the room temperature, a breaker that trips when the condenser starts, water around the air handler, or a burning-plastic smell near the electrical disconnect.

Those symptoms can point to very different parts: a capacitor, contactor, compressor, blower motor, evaporator coil, drain pan, refrigerant line, or breaker. Some faults can be isolated to a component, while others show that the existing equipment no longer makes sense to keep in service. The sound from the condenser or the temperature at one register cannot establish the scope by itself, and each possible fault carries a different amount of labor, material, and equipment work.

In-home equipment survey

During an in-home equipment survey, we inspect the existing condenser and indoor unit, read the model plate, and measure the rooms, return grille, supply vents, equipment closet, pad, line set route, drain connection, and electrical disconnect. We also check the breaker panel, the voltage supplied to the condenser, duct condition, ceiling or attic access, and the location of windows that add heat to a room. A survey commonly takes about 30 to 60 minutes, depending on whether the air handler is in a closet, attic, garage, or crawlspace.

We can usually complete the measurements, equipment review, and installation planning during that visit. If the project calls for a new condenser, indoor coil or air handler, line set, pad, disconnect, or duct alteration, the installation is scheduled after the equipment and any required permit items are arranged. A straightforward changeout can often take a full day, while a new line set route, difficult attic access, or duct changes may require another scheduled workday.

Line sets and panel access

The existing line set and its route can change the scope considerably. A clean, accessible line set of the correct size is different from copper tubing that is kinked, buried in a wall, corroded, or routed through a tight attic. If the tubing cannot be reused, the route from the indoor coil to the outdoor pad has to be planned around walls, framing, insulation, and finished surfaces.

Electrical service and equipment access also matter. A ground-level condenser beside an open gate is a different installation from a unit on a roof, behind a locked enclosure, or above a steep stairway. An older breaker panel, a seized disconnect, a damaged condenser pad, altered ductwork, equipment that must be ordered, or permit sign-off can all add steps before a new system can be put into operation.

Model plate and access path

Before calling, check the model number on the metal plate of the outdoor condenser and, if you can reach it safely, the label on the indoor air handler or furnace cabinet. Let us know which rooms stay warm, whether the system is cooling at all, and whether the breaker trips or the thermostat display is blank. It also helps to describe the equipment location, such as an attic, closet, garage, roof, or side yard, and any gate, stairway, or narrow hallway on the access path. A photo of the condenser, disconnect box, and equipment closet can help us plan the right installation scope.
