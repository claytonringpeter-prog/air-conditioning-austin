Air Conditioner Replacement in Austin, TX

We replace aging and failed air conditioning systems across Austin and nearby areas.

Call (512) 635-7760 Ask a question

When an air conditioner changes from an annoyance to a problem, the change is usually visible in the house before the system stops entirely. We see bedrooms that stay warm after the thermostat reaches its setting, supply vents that blow weakly, and condenser units that run through the afternoon without bringing the indoor temperature down. Over a week, the system may run longer and cycle more often. Over a month, a struggling compressor, blower motor, or refrigerant circuit can add wear to components that were already near the end of their service life.

We also see aging systems reach a point where one repair exposes the next weak part. A leak in an older evaporator coil, a failing compressor, or an obsolete control board can leave a homeowner deciding between another temporary repair and a new matched system. If the outdoor condenser and indoor air handler are not matched for capacity and refrigerant type, replacing only one piece may not be the right route.

We start by looking at the equipment, the duct connections, the electrical disconnect, and the rooms the system serves so we can discuss replacement based on the condition in front of us. A replacement can sometimes be planned when the unit is still cooling consistently, but a system that has stopped cooling, trips breakers, or has a compressor failure should not be left through a hot Austin season because indoor heat and extended run time will keep getting worse.

Warm air and loud noises

We hear calls about an AC that is blowing warm air, especially when the thermostat is set lower but the supply registers never feel cool. Warm air can come from a refrigerant issue, a failed compressor, a condenser fan problem, a frozen evaporator coil, or an indoor blower that is not moving enough air across the coil. We check the indoor and outdoor equipment before treating warm air as a reason to replace the whole system.

We also hear descriptions such as a loud buzzing outside, a grinding sound from the closet, a rattle at startup, or a click followed by no cooling. A buzzing condenser can point to an electrical component or compressor problem, while grinding at the air handler can involve a blower motor or wheel. Metal-on-metal noise, repeated breaker trips, and a compressor that tries but cannot start deserve a closer inspection rather than continued cycling.

Other replacement calls begin with water around the air handler, ice on the refrigerant line, a burning odor at a vent, or one side of the home that is much warmer than the rest. A clogged drain, airflow restriction, electrical fault, duct issue, and failing coil can produce overlapping symptoms. We sort out the equipment fault because the same warm room or loud unit can lead to very different parts, labor, and replacement decisions.

Equipment inspection

At an equipment inspection, we record the model and serial data from the condenser and air handler, check the thermostat operation, inspect the disconnect and breaker, and look over the refrigerant line set, condensate drain, filter slot, and visible duct connections. We also compare the temperature at return and supply registers, listen to the compressor and blower, and check whether the cabinet, coil, or wiring shows heat damage or corrosion.

A replacement survey commonly takes about 30 to 60 minutes, depending on whether the air handler is in an attic, closet, garage, or tight crawlspace. We measure the equipment cabinet, the return opening, the supply plenum, and the route for moving an old unit out and a new unit in. We also check the outdoor pad, line-set path, electrical connection, and clearance around the condenser.

During that visit, we can usually confirm the existing system type, document access conditions, and identify visible work such as a new pad, drain safety switch, disconnect, or transition piece at the ductwork. The actual equipment replacement is normally scheduled separately because the selected condenser, air handler or furnace, coil, and any required materials need to be matched and available. If an electrical upgrade, permit, or inspection sign-off applies, we include that requirement in the scope before installation work begins.

Equipment age and attic access

The age and condition of the condenser, coil, and air handler move the scope because older equipment may use discontinued parts or an older refrigerant setup that does not match current replacement equipment. We check the rating plate, the condition of the coil cabinet, and whether the indoor and outdoor sections can be replaced as a properly matched system. A unit with a readable model plate and standard connections is usually simpler to identify than equipment with missing labels, altered wiring, or previous nonstandard modifications.

Access changes the work as well. We account for attic stairs, ceiling openings, narrow hallways, closet doors, platform height, and the path between the air handler and the exterior. An attic unit behind a small access opening may require more preparation than a garage air handler with clear floor space, and a condenser set behind a gate or close to landscaping can affect removal and placement.

The refrigerant line set, electrical disconnect, condensate drain, duct transition, and permit requirements can also affect the final scope. We inspect whether the line set can be reused safely, whether the drain has proper routing, and whether the electrical components are suitable for the replacement equipment. If a required component must be ordered or an inspection sign-off is needed, those items are identified before the replacement is scheduled.

Model plate and air handler

Before calling, we ask that you look for the model number on the metal rating plate of the outdoor condenser and, if accessible, the indoor air handler or furnace cabinet. We can use a clear photo of that label to identify the equipment family, refrigerant type, and approximate age. Let us know which rooms stay warm, whether the system is running now, and whether the thermostat displays an error or keeps calling for cooling. Tell us if the air handler is in an attic, closet, garage, or crawlspace, and whether there is a narrow stairway, gate, or ceiling opening on the access route.
