AC Tune-Up in Austin, TX

AC tune-up checks for filters, coils, controls, and drainage in Austin.

Call (512) 635-7760 Ask a question

A routine tune-up and an air conditioner that has stopped cooling can both begin with a warm hallway or a thermostat that will not hold the set temperature, but they are not the same visit. A system that still cools steadily, starts normally, and has no water around the indoor unit is usually due for maintenance. Warm supply vents, repeated shutoffs, a tripped breaker, or a puddle by the air handler point to a fault that needs to be found during the check.

We handle both situations at Air Conditioning Austin. We inspect the outdoor condenser, indoor air handler, return grille, filter slot, thermostat, drain line, and electrical connections to determine whether the equipment needs routine adjustment or whether a worn component is affecting cooling.

A planned tune-up can usually wait for a convenient visit when the AC is operating normally, because its purpose is to catch dirt, drainage restrictions, and weakening electrical parts before a breakdown. A unit that is blowing warm air, leaking water, or cycling off repeatedly should not be left running, since a blocked drain, frozen coil, or failing control can worsen while the system continues to run.

Warm air and loud clicking

“My AC is running but the air is warm” is one of the most common calls for a tune-up check. The cause may be a clogged filter restricting airflow, a dirty outdoor coil releasing heat poorly, a thermostat setting issue, or a component that needs closer testing.

Customers also report loud clicking at the condenser, a fan that starts and stops, water near the indoor cabinet, or a musty smell when air comes through the supply vents. Clicking can involve a contactor or capacitor, while water often traces back to the condensate pan, drain line, or an iced evaporator coil.

Other useful symptoms include a room that stays warmer than the rest of the house, weak air from one register, short cooling cycles, and a thermostat display that goes blank. Each symptom can come from more than one part, and faults that sound alike from the hallway can require very different work and parts once we inspect the equipment.

Cooling system inspection

During a cooling system inspection, we begin at the thermostat and confirm the call for cooling, temperature response, and fan operation. We inspect the filter, return path, supply vents, indoor coil area, condensate pan, drain connection, blower compartment, outdoor coil, disconnect, and visible wiring.

We measure temperature change across the system and check operating readings against the equipment specifications. The refrigerant circuit is not judged by one universal pressure because pressure changes with the refrigerant type, outdoor temperature, indoor load, and equipment design. We also check capacitor readings, contactor condition, electrical connections, drain flow, and signs of oil or water around the equipment.

A standard inspection commonly takes about 30 to 60 minutes for one accessible system, with more time needed when the air handler is in a tight attic, the filter cabinet is difficult to reach, or the coil and drain need additional cleaning. Filter replacement, drain clearing, thermostat adjustments, and minor accessible corrections are often handled during the visit when the needed material is available. A failed motor, control board, coil, or other ordered component is documented and scheduled for a separate return visit.

Filter access and control parts

The system age matters because older condensers and air handlers may use parts that are no longer carried as common stock. A capacitor, contactor, blower control, or thermostat component that must be matched to the model can add ordering time compared with a filter or drain-line service.

Access also changes the work involved. An air handler behind a low attic opening, a condenser enclosed by shrubs, or a filter rack that cannot slide out without moving a panel takes more labor than equipment with clear working space.

The condition of the drain line, coil, disconnect, and electrical terminals affects the scope as well. A drain that clears normally is different from a line with standing water, and a disconnect or connection that is heat-damaged needs more attention than a clean, accessible component. If an electrical correction or equipment alteration requires outside approval, that requirement is discussed before related work moves forward.

Model plate and air handler

Before you call, check the model number on the equipment plate, usually found on the side of the outdoor condenser or inside the air-handler cabinet door. The model and serial label helps us identify the correct filter size, electrical components, and manufacturer-specific controls.

Let us know which room has the warmest supply vent, whether the thermostat screen is on, and whether the outdoor fan is running. It also helps to mention water at the drain pan, ice on the refrigerant line, a breaker that has tripped, or a filter that looks dark with dust.

Tell us where the indoor unit is located and whether there is clear access to the attic hatch, closet, garage, or utility room.
